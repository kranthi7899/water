# Hick's Law

A thinking tool. Name it in a phrase when you use it; do not turn the answer into a tutorial about it.

## When to use
- A decision involves the number of roughly equiprobable, simple choices presented at once.
- Not categorised search, and not choices with unequal salience or frequency.

## How to apply
1. Decision and reaction time rises with the log of the number of equiprobable options (bits of information), not linearly with the raw count.
2. This does not straightforwardly justify "fewer menu items are always faster" once options are grouped into categories, ranked by likely relevance, or unequal in how often they are picked. The original relationship is about information content, not item count.
3. If a stakeholder invokes this to argue for cutting options, check whether the choice in question is actually flat and equiprobable before applying it.

## Evidence grade
Grade A for the core information-theoretic finding (a controlled reaction-time relationship). Grade C for the popular application "fewer options is always faster" once options are categorised, unequal in probability or salience, or involve search: that generalisation is a practitioner extension, not what was experimentally established.

## Worked example
Constructed. A flat list of 30 unsorted, equally likely menu items is a genuine case: grouping or ranking them will speed selection. A search-driven command palette with a handful of frequently used, unequally likely actions at the top is not the same structural situation, and cutting its option count will not predictably speed anything up on these grounds alone.

## Source
Hick, W. E. (1952), "On the rate of gain of information", Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology 4(1), 11-26. Hyman, R. (1953), "Stimulus information as a determinant of reaction time", Journal of Experimental Psychology 45(3), 188-196.
