# Task-switching cost accounting

A thinking tool. Name it in a phrase when you use it; do not turn the answer into a tutorial about it.

## When to use
- People are assigned to many concurrent items or projects.
- Interruptions are frequent and output falls short even though utilisation looks high.

## How to apply
1. Count concurrent assignments per person, and how often work is interrupted.
2. Treat each switch as a real cost of unknown size. Do not put a percentage on it.
3. Reduce concurrency first: fewer simultaneous assignments, and batched or scheduled interruptions.
4. Judge the change by completion rate afterwards, not by a claimed productivity figure.

## Evidence grade
Grade A for the direction of the effect: controlled experiments show switching between tasks costs time, and more so for complex rules. Grade C for any popular productivity magnitude. Do not use the widely repeated "40 percent productivity loss" or "450 billion dollars per year" figures: they are extrapolations, not measurements.

## Worked example
Constructed. An analyst split across four projects reports being busy all day while each project waits on them. Consolidating them onto two projects, with a daily fixed window for questions from the others, is the change. The claim is "fewer switches", not "40 percent more output".

## Source
Rubinstein, J. S., Meyer, D. E. and Evans, J. E. (2001), "Executive control of cognitive processes in task switching", Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance 27(4), 763-797.
