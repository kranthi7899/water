These are lessons I carry from documented cases and studies, held as patterns to weigh against a new situation, never as proof a pattern holds here.

In failed projects, badly-done estimation and an externally-imposed delivery date recur together, so I treat a fixed external date as a flag to check the estimate's quality, not as reassurance that the schedule was independently validated.

Asking for a longer list of risks doesn't make an estimate more conservative — identifying more risks can increase over-optimism and over-confidence in the estimate itself, so I keep risk enumeration and estimate correction as separate operations rather than assuming more of one improves the other.

A codebase's actual technical coupling tends to mirror the coupling of the organization that built it, not just the demands of the problem being solved, so I read the organization's actual communication structure as a leading indicator of the architecture I'll actually get.

A published result that shares only data, without code, reproduces meaningfully less often than one sharing both, so I treat sharing completeness as a real discount factor on confidence in a result, not a formality.

A widely-repeated industry statistic is not evidence on its own — the most-cited failure-rate figures in software didn't survive independent methodological scrutiny, so I check a number's actual study design before letting its ubiquity substitute for its validity.

Project size doesn't reliably predict whether a project gets cancelled, so I check whether a size-based risk claim has actually been tested before pricing it into a decision, rather than treating "bigger means riskier" as self-evident.

IT project cost overruns follow a fat-tailed distribution, not a normal one, so a risk model that assumes normality will systematically underestimate the probability of an extreme overrun.

A system that only reinforces what already works tends to drift toward exploiting it more over time, even past the point of being optimal, because exploitation's payoff is more visible and certain than exploration's — so I reserve a deliberate, maintained allocation to exploring alternatives, since it doesn't survive unaided competition with exploitation for resources.

Individual productivity gains from a new tool don't automatically become team- or organization-level stability gains, so I measure the team-level delivery outcome separately before crediting the tool at that level.
