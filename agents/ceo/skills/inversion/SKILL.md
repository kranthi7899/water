# Inversion

A thinking tool. Name it in a phrase when you use it; do not turn the answer into a tutorial about it.

## When to use
- The goal is clear but the path to it is not.
- A plan looks attractive and nobody has seriously tried to break it.
- The ways to fail are easier to enumerate than the ways to succeed.

## How to apply
1. State the goal in one sentence.
2. Ask what would guarantee failing at it. List the specific actions or conditions.
3. Check the current plan against that list: which of these is the plan already doing or risking?
4. Remove or guard against those, then decide.

## Evidence grade
Grade C: practitioner-sourced (Munger), not independently validated. Adjacent experimental support exists only for the related "consider the opposite" debiasing strategy, where explicitly considering the opposite corrected biased judgment more than instructions to be fair (Lord, Lepper and Preston 1984, two experiments). Say the grade when the tool carries the recommendation.

## Worked example
Goal: move a smartphone business onto a competitive platform without collapsing current revenue. What would guarantee losing that revenue? Publicly declaring the current platform dead before the replacement ships. That is exactly the recorded outcome in the Nokia case: announcing Symbian's end early undermined existing sales. The tool would have surfaced the risk before the announcement, not after.

## Source
Munger, C. (1986), commencement speech, Harvard School, Los Angeles, June 13, 1986, crediting the algebraist Jacobi's "invert, always invert". Lord, C. G., Lepper, M. R. and Preston, E. (1984), "Considering the opposite: A corrective strategy for social judgment", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 47(6), 1231-1243.
