# Aggregated heuristic evaluation (Nielsen's ten), conditional

A thinking tool. Name it in a phrase when you use it; do not turn the answer into a tutorial about it.

## When to use
- A fast, low-cost inspection of an interface is wanted before or instead of user testing.

## How to apply
1. Inspect the interface against the ten heuristics.
2. Use 3 to 5 independent evaluators and aggregate their findings. Never treat a single pass, including this role's own, as an adequate or exhaustive review.
3. State single-pass coverage honestly when only one pass was possible, rather than implying the review was comprehensive.

## Caveat that must be carried into every use
Across the comparative studies Hertzum and Jacobsen synthesise, individual evaluators detected roughly 20 to 51 percent of the problems present, singly, and different evaluators substantially disagreed about which problems those were. This evaluator effect held for heuristic evaluation, cognitive walkthrough, and think-aloud testing alike. Cite the 20 to 51 percent range as the headline number.

## Evidence grade
Grade C for the heuristics list itself: a practitioner synthesis, not independently validated as a set. Grade B for the caveat that makes it usable responsibly: peer-reviewed research on how much any single evaluator's pass actually finds.

## Worked example
Constructed. A single-pass review by this role must state "this is one evaluator's pass; single-evaluator coverage in the published research runs roughly 20 to 51 percent and is evaluator-dependent" rather than presenting itself as an exhaustive audit.

## Source
Nielsen, J. and Molich, R. (1990); Nielsen, J. (1994), origin of the ten heuristics (Nielsen Norman Group). Hertzum, M. and Jacobsen, N. E. (2003), "The Evaluator Effect: A Chilling Fact About Usability Evaluation Methods", International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction 15(1), 183-204.
